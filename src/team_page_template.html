How it works

How the computer listened, step by step

1

Record the sky

A recorder sat in a bucket, pointing straight up, from evening until morning. Each night made a few huge sound files. Listening to all of them ourselves would take almost 3 days without sleeping!

2

Clean up the sound

Crickets and katydids buzz all night on the same notes. Cars and wind rumble low. A bird's flight call is quick: about one tenth of a second. So the computer turns down every sound that stays the same for a long time, and keeps the quick ones. Listen to the same moment before and after:

Before: what the microphone heard

After: cleaned up

See the bright line at the bottom and the stripes near 4 kHz in the "before" picture? That's road rumble and crickets. In the "after" picture, only the bird (an American Redstart) is left.

3

Four computer "ears" listen

We asked four different computer programs to listen to every second. Each one gives a score: how sure it is that a bird is calling.

Ours

Our own detector

We trained it ourselves on 35,000 night calls that scientists marked. Best at quick night calls.

Scientists' tool

Nighthawk

Made by bird scientists just for night flight calls. Knows 82 kinds of birds.

Cornell Lab

BirdNET

Knows over 6,000 sounds, mostly songs that birds sing in the daytime.

Google

Perch v2

Knows birds, frogs and insects from all over the world.

4

A referee decides: bird or not?

The four ears don't always agree. A small "referee" program studied a night that scientists had already checked, call by call. It learned how much to trust each ear. Now it turns their four scores into one answer.

5

The ears vote on the bird's name

Each ear says which bird it thinks it is. When two, three or all four pick the same bird, we trust the name more. In the dark, the night-call experts get the biggest votes. In the morning, the song experts do. We also trained a second program of our own that learned the calls of 25 kinds of sparrows and warblers.

6

Cut short clips for the team to check

Every bird sound was cut into its own short file, cleaned up and made louder so it's easy to hear. All __CLIPS__ clips are saved next to our recordings, with a table that tells the time and every computer's guess. Now it's our turn to be the judges.

Example name: 100626-193652-6772-6775_auto.WAV = recording 100626-193652, from second 6772 to second 6775.

What we used

How did the computer learn birds?

Computers learn a bit like we learn with flash cards. You show them thousands of examples with the right answer, and they slowly figure out the pattern. Here is what we gave ours:

35,000

night calls marked by scientists

BirdVox: 6 fall nights recorded in New York state. Experts marked every flight call they heard.

85,000

short calls with bird names

BirdVox-ANAFCC: calls of sparrows, warblers and more from the American Northeast, each labelled with its bird.

23 min

to train our detector

A gaming computer with a strong graphics card practiced on those calls 20 times over.

3 hours

to listen to all our nights

The four computer ears listened about 20 times faster than real time.

Results

The computer's report card

Does it find the birds?

97 out of every 100 calls

On a test night the computer had never heard, scientists had marked 6,839 calls. Our team's computer found 97 out of every 100.

When it says "bird!", is it right?

~80 out of every 100 clips

We looked at 36 random night clips from our own recordings. About 4 out of 5 were real bird calls. The rest were insects or too faint to tell. That's why we need human judges!

Our own detector found the most of the team's calls

Still learning

What still fools the computer

Katydids

A row of chirps that repeat like a ticking clock is an insect. Our computer sometimes calls it a bird.

The dawn chorus

Around sunrise many birds sing at once. The computer names the loudest one, maybe not the one you hear first.

Very quiet calls

Birds flying very high are faint. Some calls are too quiet for the computer, and for us.

Low honks

Our detector listens for high, quick calls. It missed the team's Canada Goose honk because it's low.

Word bank

New words

Migration
A long trip birds take each spring and fall. Many songbirds fly at night.
Flight call
A very short sound a bird makes while flying, maybe to keep in touch with the flock.
Spectrogram
A picture of sound. Time goes left to right, high notes are at the top, loud sounds are bright.
kHz
Kilohertz, the unit for how high a note is. Most flight calls are between 2 and 10 kHz. The middle C on a piano is only about 0.26 kHz.
AI model
A computer program that learned a skill from lots of examples instead of being told the rules.
Training
Showing the model thousands of examples with the right answers, again and again, until it gets good.
Denoise
Cleaning up a recording by turning down the noise and keeping the interesting sounds.
False alarm
When the computer says "bird!" but it was really an insect, a car or nothing.
